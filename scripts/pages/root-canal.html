When a tooth needs a root canal

Inside every tooth is a soft core — the pulp — containing the nerve and blood supply. When decay, a crack or a heavy knock lets bacteria reach that pulp, it becomes inflamed and then infected. The infection cannot heal on its own, because there is no way for the body's immune system to clear bacteria from inside a sealed tooth.

Root canal treatment removes the infected pulp, disinfects and shapes the canals it leaves behind, and seals them so bacteria cannot return. The tooth stays in place and keeps doing its job.

Signs you might need one

  • Pain that lingers for more than a few seconds after something hot or cold
  • Throbbing that gets worse when you lie down, or wakes you at night
  • Pain when biting down on one particular tooth
  • A pimple-like swelling on the gum near a tooth, which may taste unpleasant
  • A tooth that has gone noticeably darker than its neighbours
  • Swelling of the face or jaw — this needs to be seen urgently

Not every infected tooth hurts. If the nerve has already died, the pain can stop completely while the infection quietly continues spreading into the bone. That is why a tooth that stopped hurting on its own still needs checking.

What actually happens during the procedure

  1. X-ray and diagnosis. A digital X-ray shows the shape of the roots and how far the infection has spread into the surrounding bone.
  2. Local anaesthetic. The tooth and the surrounding gum are numbed thoroughly before anything else begins.
  3. Isolating the tooth. A rubber dam is placed so the tooth stays dry and clean, and nothing goes down your throat.
  4. Cleaning the canals. A small opening is made in the top of the tooth, the infected pulp is removed, and the canals are shaped with fine instruments and flushed with disinfectant solution.
  5. Sealing. The canals are filled with a rubber-like material called gutta-percha and sealed so bacteria cannot re-enter.
  6. Rebuilding the tooth. The access hole is filled, and in most cases a crown is fitted afterwards.

Single sitting or more than one visit?

Many root canals can be completed in a single appointment. Where there is active infection, a lot of pus, or the canals are unusually curved or difficult, splitting the treatment across two or three visits gives the disinfectant time to work between appointments and produces a more reliable result. Which applies to your tooth is a clinical judgement made after the X-ray — we'll tell you before we start, not halfway through.

Does it hurt?

The procedure itself is done under local anaesthetic, and for most people it feels similar to having a deep filling. The reputation root canals have comes largely from the pain of the infection that leads to them — treatment is what relieves that pain, not what causes it.

Afterwards, the tooth is usually tender to bite on for a few days while the ligament around the root settles. Ordinary painkillers handle this. Severe pain, swelling, or pain that is getting worse rather than better after the first two or three days is not expected, and you should call us.

Why the tooth usually needs a crown afterwards

A tooth that has had a root canal has lost its blood supply and usually a fair amount of its structure — both to the original decay and to the access opening. It becomes more brittle, and back teeth in particular are at real risk of splitting under chewing force. A split root generally cannot be repaired, and the tooth is then lost.

A crown wraps the remaining tooth and holds it together. Skipping it to save money frequently costs more later, because the alternative to a cracked root is an implant or a bridge. Front teeth with small access openings are sometimes the exception.

What if you don't treat it

An untreated infected tooth does not get better. The infection spreads through the tip of the root into the jawbone, forming an abscess. That can lead to bone loss around the tooth, facial swelling, and in serious cases infection spreading into the spaces of the neck and face, which is a medical emergency. The realistic alternative to a root canal is not "leave it" — it is extraction.

Common questions

How long does root canal treatment take?

A single-sitting root canal on a front tooth typically takes about 45 to 60 minutes. Molars have more canals and usually take 60 to 90 minutes. If treatment is split across visits, each appointment is shorter and they are usually spaced about a week apart.

Is a root canal better than pulling the tooth out?

In almost all cases, yes. Keeping your own tooth preserves the bone around it and the natural bite. Once a tooth is removed, the neighbouring teeth start drifting and the bone in that area gradually shrinks, so replacing it later with an implant or bridge becomes more complicated and more expensive than the root canal would have been.

How long does a root-treated tooth last?

A well-done root canal that is properly crowned and kept clean can last for decades. The main reasons treated teeth fail are fracture from not being crowned, new decay at the margins, and infection that was not fully cleared. Regular check-ups catch all three early.

Can I eat and go back to work afterwards?

Yes, most people return to work the same day. Avoid chewing on the treated side until the numbness has completely worn off, so you don't bite your cheek or lip. If a temporary filling is in place, stick to softer food on that side until the permanent restoration is fitted.

Do you offer root canal treatment for children?

Baby teeth can have a similar procedure, called a pulpectomy, when decay reaches the nerve. Keeping a baby molar until it is ready to fall out naturally matters, because it holds the space for the adult tooth coming through behind it.
