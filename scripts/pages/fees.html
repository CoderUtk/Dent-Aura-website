Why we don't publish a single price per treatment

"How much is a root canal?" is a completely reasonable question, and the honest answer is that it depends on the tooth. A front tooth has one canal; a lower molar usually has three or four, sometimes more, and takes twice as long. Quoting one number for both would mean overcharging some people and undercharging others.

What we can do is be clear about what drives the cost, and give you a written estimate before any treatment begins — not after.

Ask for the estimate up front. Before you agree to anything, you should know the total, what it includes, whether a crown or follow-up visit is extra, and what happens to the price if something unexpected is found. If a clinic won't put that in writing, that tells you something.

What changes the price

Which tooth it is

Back teeth cost more than front teeth for almost every procedure. They have more roots, more canals, less access, and they take the heaviest chewing forces, so the restoration on top has to be stronger.

How much of the tooth is left

A tooth with a large cavity may need a post and core built into it before a crown can be fitted. A tooth caught earlier may need only a filling. The same named procedure can involve quite different amounts of work.

The material you choose

Crowns range from metal-ceramic through to full zirconia and full ceramic. They differ in strength, in how natural they look, and in price. For a back molar that nobody sees, the strongest sensible option is usually the right call; for a front tooth, appearance matters more. We'll explain the trade-off rather than just quoting the most expensive one.

Whether anything has to happen first

Gum disease is treated before orthodontics. Thin bone may need grafting before an implant. These are separate stages with separate costs, and they should be in the plan from the beginning so nothing appears as a surprise later.

Number of visits

A root canal completed in one sitting and one split across three appointments cost broadly similarly overall, but they spread differently. If paying in stages matters to you, say so — it can be planned around.

Getting an estimate before you commit

  1. Message or call us and describe the problem. For many things we can give you a ballpark straight away.
  2. Come in for an examination. This is where an X-ray shows what's actually going on — guessing from a photo isn't diagnosis.
  3. You get a written plan listing each stage, what it costs, and what is and isn't included.
  4. Decide in your own time. Except in genuine emergencies, there's no reason to commit on the spot.

Second opinions

If you've been quoted for extensive work somewhere else and it doesn't sit right with you, bring the treatment plan and any X-rays. A second opinion on a large plan is worth the consultation fee, whichever way it goes.

Common questions

Do I have to pay for everything up front?

No. Multi-stage treatment such as implants or orthodontics is normally paid in stages as the work progresses. Talk to us about how you'd like to spread it before treatment starts and we'll plan around it.

Is the consultation charged separately?

Yes, an examination and diagnosis is its own appointment with its own fee, because it involves clinical time and usually X-rays. Ask us for the current consultation fee when you book — it's a small, fixed amount and we'll tell you before you come in.

Will my insurance cover it?

Most Indian health insurance policies cover dental treatment only when it follows an accident or is medically necessary, not routine or cosmetic work. Check your specific policy. We can provide itemised invoices and treatment records for any claim you want to make.

Can the price change once treatment starts?

Occasionally something is only visible once we're working — a crack under an old filling, or a canal that's calcified. If that happens we stop, explain what's changed and what it means for the cost, and you decide before we go any further. You should never find out about extra cost from the bill.
