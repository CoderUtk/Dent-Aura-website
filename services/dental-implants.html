What a dental implant actually is

An implant is a small titanium screw placed into the jawbone where a tooth root used to be. Over the following months, bone grows onto its surface and locks it in place — a process called osseointegration. Once that has happened, a connector and a crown are fitted on top.

The result is a replacement tooth that stands on its own. It doesn't rest on the teeth beside it, and it doesn't come out at night.

The three parts

  • The fixture — the titanium screw inside the bone, which you never see
  • The abutment — the connector that passes through the gum
  • The crown — the visible tooth, usually zirconia or metal-ceramic

Implants compared with bridges and dentures

There are three realistic ways to replace a missing tooth, and the right one depends on your bone, your other teeth and your budget.

An implant leaves the neighbouring teeth untouched and preserves the bone in that area, because the bone is still being loaded when you chew. It costs the most up front and takes the longest.

A bridge is faster — usually two to three weeks — and costs less. The trade-off is that the healthy teeth on either side have to be filed down to carry it. If those teeth already need crowns for other reasons, that trade-off is much easier to accept.

A removable denture is the least expensive and can replace several teeth at once, but it moves while you eat, needs taking out to clean, and does nothing to stop the bone underneath shrinking over the years.

Bone loss after an extraction is fastest in the first year. If you already know you want an implant, mentioning it before a tooth is removed lets us plan the extraction to preserve as much bone as possible.

Who is suitable

Most healthy adults are. The things that genuinely matter are:

  • Enough bone. If the tooth has been missing for years, the ridge may have shrunk. Bone grafting can rebuild it, which adds time and cost.
  • Healthy gums. Active gum disease has to be treated first — implants are vulnerable to the same kind of infection around them that teeth are.
  • Controlled diabetes. Poorly controlled blood sugar slows healing and raises the risk of the implant failing to integrate.
  • Not smoking. Smoking substantially increases implant failure rates. Stopping, even just around the surgery and healing period, makes a real difference.
  • Finished growing. Implants don't move as the jaw develops, so they're generally not placed in teenagers until growth is complete.

A cone-beam or standard X-ray tells us how much bone is there before anything is decided.

The treatment timeline

  1. Assessment. X-rays, a look at your bite and gums, and a written plan with costs.
  2. Extraction or grafting, if needed. Where a failing tooth is still present or bone is thin, this comes first and heals for a few months.
  3. Placing the implant. Done under local anaesthetic, usually 30 to 60 minutes for a single implant. Most people describe it as easier than the extraction was.
  4. Healing. Roughly three to six months for the bone to integrate, depending on the site and your healing. A temporary tooth can usually be worn during this time.
  5. Fitting the crown. Impressions or a scan are taken, the crown is made, and it's fitted and adjusted to your bite.

Start to finish, a straightforward single implant is commonly four to six months. Cases needing grafting run longer.

Looking after implants

Implants can't decay, but the gum and bone around them can become infected — peri-implantitis — and that is the main reason implants are lost years after placement. Brushing, cleaning between the teeth daily, and regular professional cleaning are what keep an implant for the long term. They are not maintenance-free teeth.

Common questions

Is getting an implant painful?

The placement is done under local anaesthetic, so you feel pressure but not pain. Most people find the discomfort afterwards milder than they expected — comparable to an extraction — and manageable with ordinary painkillers for two or three days. Some swelling and bruising is normal in the first 48 hours.

How long do dental implants last?

The titanium fixture itself is designed to last decades and often lasts a lifetime. The crown on top is the part that wears and typically needs replacing after ten to fifteen years. Long-term survival depends far more on gum health and cleaning than on the implant itself.

Can I get the implant and the tooth on the same day?

Sometimes. Immediate loading — placing a temporary crown on the implant the same day — is possible when the bone is dense and the implant is very stable at placement. It is not suitable for everyone, and forcing it where the bone isn't right risks losing the implant. We assess this case by case.

What if I've lost several teeth?

You don't need one implant per missing tooth. Two implants can support a three-tooth bridge, and a full arch can often be restored on four to six implants. This is usually more affordable per tooth than individual implants.

Will anyone be able to tell?

A well-made implant crown is shaded and shaped to match the teeth around it, and because it emerges through the gum like a natural tooth, it generally isn't distinguishable in normal conversation. Front teeth are the most demanding, since the gum line has to match the other side.
